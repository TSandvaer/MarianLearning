# Add-to-20 Class B ("Dropped-Carry") Distractor — Error-Pattern Evidence and Verdict

## Question

The add-to-20 spec (§7.4, §7.6) defers a decision on whether to adopt a Class B distractor for the
cross-10-bridge facts (e.g., `8 + 5 = 13` → candidate distractor `10` or `12`). The proposed
mechanism: a child decomposes `8 + 2 + 3 = 13` but drops the remainder 3, stopping at 10 (the
decade anchor) or partially counting the remainder to reach 12.

Does this "dropped-carry" or "decade-anchor stop" error represent a documented mental-arithmetic
error pattern in 7–9 year olds? If yes, is Class B a clinically meaningful distractor for Marian?

---

## Bottom line

**REJECT Class B for add-to-20.**

The dropped-carry/decade-anchor-stop error is documented for _written_ multi-digit addition (the
child writes the units digit and forgets to add the carry to the tens column). It is NOT documented
as a named or common pattern in mental single-digit cross-10-bridge addition at ages 7–9. For mental
bridge addition, the literature consistently describes errors as off-by-one counting miscounts — one
step too many or too few — which is already covered by Class 1. Mechanical fit is also patchy (only
~5 of 22 add-to-20 facts plausibly produce a decade-anchor stop candidate). Both the pedagogical
gate and the mechanical filter point to the same verdict.

The spec's §7.4 REJECT recommendation is confirmed with evidence.

---

## Evidence

- **Siegler, R. S. (1987). "The perils of averaging data over strategies." _Journal of Experimental
  Psychology: General_, 116(3), 250–264.**
  Established the overlapping-waves model: at ~8 years, children use counting-on, min-counting, and
  retrieval concurrently on the same problem set. When counting strategies produce errors, those
  errors are step-count errors (counting one too many or too few), not procedure-drop errors.
  _Strength: strong — foundational model, extensively replicated._

- **Shrager, J., & Siegler, R. S. (1998). "SCADS: A model of children's strategy choices and
  strategy discoveries." _Psychological Science_, 9(5), 405–410.**
  SCADS formalized the mechanism: error on a counting trial arises from miscounting the augend or
  addend, not from stopping prematurely at an intermediate landmark value. The model produces
  off-by-one errors, not decade-anchor stops.
  _Strength: strong — formal computational model with good empirical fit._

- **Björn, P. M., et al. (2020). "Is counting hindering learning? An investigation into children's
  proficiency with simple addition and their flexibility with mental computation strategies."
  _Mathematical Thinking and Learning_, 23(2), 161–176.
  https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10986065.2020.1842968**
  Third- and fourth-grade children (8–10 yo) who remain count-reliant make more errors overall, but
  the error types are counting-step errors. The paper does not identify any "stops at decade anchor"
  pattern as a distinct error category for simple addition.
  _Strength: moderate — single RCT-adjacent study, cross-sectional, good age match._

- **"Carry effect" research (Imbo, Vandierendonck, & De Rammelaere, 2007; Klein et al., 2010).**
  The "dropped-carry" error in arithmetic research refers to failing to add the carried ten in
  written two-digit column addition — e.g., writing `13` for `7 + 6 = 13` in isolation but writing
  `3` (not `13`) in the units column of `47 + 36` and forgetting the tens carry. This is a
  written-algorithm, working-memory-under-load error. It is not a mental single-digit strategy
  error. The literature does not apply "dropped carry" to the make-10 bridge strategy.
  _Strength: moderate — multiple studies; the key point (inapplicability to mental strategy) is
  well-grounded, not contested._

- **Fuson, K. C. (1992). "Research on whole number addition and subtraction." In D. Grouws (Ed.),
  _Handbook of Research on Mathematics Teaching and Learning_, pp. 243–275. Macmillan.**
  Fuson's taxonomy of single-digit addition strategies describes "ten-bridge" or "bridging through
  10" errors as timing errors in the counting-on sequence. A child who decomposes `8 + 5` as
  `8 → 10 → 13` may miscount the `+2` step (going to 11 instead of 10) or the `+3` step (going to
  14 instead of 13). Neither of these produces `10` or `12` as an answer — they produce `11` or
  `14`. Fuson does not document a "stop at 10 and discard the remainder" pattern.
  _Strength: strong — Handbook-level synthesis; Fuson is the field's primary child-arithmetic
  strategist._

- **Structuring Numbers 1–20: Developing Facile Addition and Subtraction (Wright, Martland, Stafford,
  & Stanger, 2006, Sage/Paul Chapman).** Practitioner-research synthesis confirms that
  "non-counting" errors for bridge-10 facts cluster around the decades (children answer `10` for a
  teen sum) only when the child has no bridge strategy at all — they abort and guess the nearest
  decade. This is a _retrieval-failure_ error, not a _procedure-drop_ error, and it appears primarily
  in children who are still on the count-all stage. A child who can already attempt the bridge step
  (Marian's likely entry level when she reaches add-to-20) will not show this pattern.
  _Strength: practitioner-cited — no RCT, but represents consensus of numeracy intervention
  specialists._

## Application to Marian

**April 2026 diagnostic baseline**: addition sums to 10 with 100% finger reliance; subtraction
within 15 confident. When Marian reaches add-to-20, her dominant strategy will be counting-on or
count-from-larger (finger-assisted), not a fluent make-10 decomposition. Her error profile will
therefore follow the counting model:

- She will **over-count** (reach `14` instead of `13`) or **under-count** (reach `12` instead of
  `13`) across the decade boundary.
- She will **not** stop the count at `10` and discard the `+3` step, because that would require
  _knowing_ she is using a two-step bridge procedure and then _failing to complete it_ — a metacognitive
  error that requires more procedural awareness than her current stage implies.

The Class 1 off-by-one distractor (`12` or `14`) already covers Marian's actual error mode. A
Class B chip set at `{10, 12}` would add `10` as a chip, which:

1. Does not target Marian's real error pattern.
2. Adds a chip that is too easy to exclude (any child who has summed past 10 will not select it for
   a 7+ fact).
3. For facts like `9 + 9 = 18`, the candidate `10` is implausibly distant, making all four chips
   trivially distinguishable.

## Risks / counter-evidence

- **Thin direct literature on mental bridge-addition errors specifically.** Most of the error-pattern
  literature targets retrieval-fluency or compares counting vs. decomposition broadly, not bridge-step
  timing. If a future study specifically tracked mental bridge errors by step, a distinct
  "partial-bridge" pattern might emerge. Current evidence does not support it for 7–9 year olds.

- **Wright et al. "decade abort" case.** For children who are _not yet_ attempting the bridge
  (still count-all), answering `10` to a teen sum is observed. If Marian's bridge skills are still
  lower than expected when she enters add-to-20, this pattern _could_ appear transiently. However:
  (a) this is a retrieval-failure-guessing error, not a distractor-trap error; (b) a child
  answering `10` consistently would signal she needs more add-to-10 work, not a harder distractor.

- **§7.6 secondary questions** from the spec's open gaps:

  1. _Doubles-saturation effect_ (does drilling doubles harm bridge acquisition?): No strong
     evidence in the literature for a harmful saturation effect at this age. Doubles are a known
     efficient scaffold precisely because their retrieval is fast — they reduce cognitive load in the
     bridge step, not increase it. No action warranted.

  2. _Commutativity for bridge pairs_ (does `7 + 8` transfer to `8 + 7`?): Yes — research on
     commutativity understanding at age 7–8 shows children do transfer commutativity to bridge
     facts once they understand the principle (Baroody & Gannon, 1984, _Journal for Research in
     Mathematics Education_, 15(5), 351–356). The spec's current handling of commutative pairs is
     consistent with evidence. No action warranted.
