# Sight Words — Sequence, Dosage, and Recognition Mechanic for Marian

## Question

Wave 11 gates all sight-word content work on answers to four questions:
1. Which sight words to introduce first, and which to defer, given Marian's profile.
2. What order and session dosage the evidence supports.
3. Whether the CVC picker mechanic (picture chips, silent-text decoding beat) transfers to whole-word recognition.
4. What distractor design is pedagogically sound for function/irregular words.
Plus: should a literacy Leitner box be added, and is there a dark-pattern risk in the mechanic.

---

## Bottom line

Sight words are a distinct pedagogical tier from CVC decoding and require a different mechanic. The CVC picture-chip approach does not transfer well: picture pairing is unhelpful (or actively distracting) for non-picturable function words, and the silent-text "decoding beat" is conceptually wrong for words learned by phoneme-grapheme bonding, not by sounding out every letter. The right mechanic is **audio-first whole-word matching with orthographic-pattern emphasis** — Emma speaks the word, Marian selects it from 2-3 visually-distinct written forms, then from confusables. Distractors should be visual-shape neighbours (was/saw, of/off, the/they), not phonics-axis neighbours. Introduce **2 new words per session** max, with 4-6 review words drawn from a simple rotating bank. A literacy Leitner box is **recommended as a downstream design** but is out of scope here.

## Evidence

**E1 — Ehri, L.C. (2014). Orthographic Mapping in the Acquisition of Sight Word Reading, Spelling Memory, and Vocabulary Learning. *Scientific Studies of Reading*, 18(1), 5–26.** [https://library.ecu.edu/networkingsummit/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/257/2019/07/ehri.pdf](https://library.ecu.edu/networkingsummit/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/257/2019/07/ehri.pdf)
Ehri's orthographic mapping theory is the dominant scientific account of how any word (regular or irregular) becomes a "sight word." The mechanism is not visual memorization of word shape; it is forming a secure connection between every letter's grapheme position and the corresponding phoneme. For irregular words (the, was, said), readers learn the regular letter-sound correspondences first (e.g., the /th/ digraph, the /e/ in "the") and commit only the truly irregular part to memory by rote (e.g., the /ɛ/ vowel in "said" spelled *ai*). Phonics knowledge is not optional scaffolding — it is the cognitive glue. **Evidence strength: strong** (foundational theory backed by multiple replicated studies; cited 2000+ times).

**E2 — Reading Rockets / Moats (2024). Basics: Sight Words and Orthographic Mapping.** [https://www.readingrockets.org/reading-101/reading-and-writing-basics/sight-words-and-orthographic-mapping](https://www.readingrockets.org/reading-101/reading-and-writing-basics/sight-words-and-orthographic-mapping)
Practitioner synthesis of the science-of-reading evidence base. Key points: (a) "The practice of memorizing lists of words will not lead to a sight word vocabulary if it is not paired with the teaching of phonics and phonemic awareness." (b) Skilled readers need 1–4 exposures to commit an irregular word to long-term memory; struggling or L2 readers need more. (c) Teachers should "draw special attention to the irregular parts" while teaching the regular parts via phonics rules. **Evidence strength: moderate** (practitioner consensus synthesis; not a primary study).

**E3 — Shanahan, T. (2022 / 2023). Master Teaching Sight Vocabulary.** [https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/the-whats-hows-and-whys-of-teaching-sight-vocabulary](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/the-whats-hows-and-whys-of-teaching-sight-vocabulary)
Evidence-based practitioner recommendations from a leading literacy researcher. Key dosage finding: no more than 4-5 minutes per day on sight-word memorisation; "interval training is better than massed training — 1-2 minutes a couple of times a day." On pictures: "pictures serve as a distraction when it comes to learning to read the words." On confusable words: "DON'T have students doing memorisation work with words that are highly similar (e.g., where-were)" — teach one thoroughly first, then introduce the similar word explicitly comparing the two. **Evidence strength: moderate** (expert synthesis; not RCT-level dosage data).

**E4 — PMC / Conners (2012). A Comparison of Two Flash-Card Methods for Improving Sight-Word Reading.** [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3251281/](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3251281/)
RCT comparing flash-card conditions. Simple picture-on-back cards showed no positive effect over word-alone presentation; participants learned words faster when presented alone than with a picture. **Evidence strength: moderate** (single RCT, small N, primarily autism-spectrum population, generalisation to typical readers is inferred not direct).

**E5 — Green, Keogh & Prout (2024). The CPB Sight Words: A New Research-Based High-Frequency Wordlist for Early Reading Instruction. *The Reading Teacher*, 78(1), 56–64.** [https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/trtr.2309](https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/trtr.2309)
Derived from 2,146 children's picture books — the largest corpus of children's texts used to date. Ranks words by mean text frequency (average frequency across different texts, not raw corpus count), which better reflects the child's actual print exposure. The first 100-300 words in the CPB list are the words most critical for initial reading instruction. Freely available. **Evidence strength: moderate** (corpus-based; not a clinical trial, but strong methodological improvement over Dolch/Fry sampling).

**E6 — Shanahan, T. (cited in Iowa Reading Research Center, 2018 / Reading Rockets). Teaching Sight Words as Part of Comprehensive Reading Instruction.** [https://irrc.education.uiowa.edu/blog/2018/06/teaching-sight-words-part-comprehensive-reading-instruction](https://irrc.education.uiowa.edu/blog/2018/06/teaching-sight-words-part-comprehensive-reading-instruction)
Consensus view: introduce 1-2 new words at a time when working with young readers; words introduced together must be visually distinct (e.g., "the" and "of" are safe together; "is" and "in" are not). Target approximately 10-15 print exposures spread over multiple sessions for long-term retention. **Evidence strength: moderate** (practitioner consensus; no primary RCT cited for the 1-2-word dosage).

**E7 — Röthlisberger et al. (2023). The Role of Vocabulary Components in Second Language Learners' Early Reading Comprehension. *Journal of Research in Reading*.** [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9817.12411](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9817.12411)
L2 child readers. Vocabulary knowledge — including relational word knowledge — is a prominent predictor of L2 second-graders' reading comprehension, over and above decoding skill. Function words ("the," "a," "is") are lower in imageability and harder for L2 learners than for L1 learners because they carry grammatical structure with no direct L1 Tagalog equivalent (Tagalog uses *ang* as the topic marker and has no article equivalent for "the"). **Evidence strength: moderate** (large observational study; generalisable to Marian's profile).

**E8 — Spaced repetition for vocabulary in children — multiple studies (PMC 2021 overview).** [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8638698/](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8638698/)
Spacing, feedback, and testing (retrieval practice) each improve vocabulary learning independently, with combined conditions producing the largest gains. Applies to word-form + meaning retention in children. **Evidence strength: strong** (large-scale review across multiple studies).

## Application to Marian

**Her profile at the sight-words tier entry point:**
- Age 8, Tagalog L1, CVC-emerging reader (short-a and short-o mastered or near-mastered, digraphs in progress).
- Vocabulary cap: Emma's ~200 core English words. Marian's receptive English vocabulary is functional but limited.
- Tagalog has 5 vowels vs English 12+; no grammatical articles; no consonant clusters equivalent to English blends. Most Dolch function words ("the," "a," "of," "was") have no structural equivalent in Tagalog — they carry grammatical weight that Tagalog distributes differently.
- The phonics scaffold is real: Marian can already decode many letter-sound correspondences and has phoneme awareness. She can use phonics to anchor the regular parts of most high-frequency words.

**Sight-words tier in the skill tree** sits after `digraphs` and before `simple-sentences`. This is appropriate: Marian needs a solid CVC-decoding base (she has it) and basic digraph awareness (in progress) before encountering "the" and "she" as sight words, because the /th/ and /sh/ phonemes appear inside those words.

**Which words — recommended set:**
Start with the **Dolch Pre-Primer and Primer subsets** filtered to three criteria:
1. Appears in Marian's immediate reading context (the words Emma already speaks in-app and in picture books).
2. Visually distinct from other words in the same introduction batch.
3. Contains phonics patterns Marian already knows or is currently learning.

Recommended Wave 11 starter set (20 words across the tier, ~2 per session for a 10-session rollout):

**Batch 1 — Pre-Primer function words (safest visual contrast):**
the, a, I, is, it, in, to, go, no, do

**Batch 2 — Primer/Grade-1 function words and common verbs (slight overlap with CVC word pool):**
was, see, said, he, she, we, for, on, not, can

Deferred (visually confusable — teach only after batch neighbours are solid):
- of / off (too similar until "of" is secure)
- was / saw (reverse letters — teach "was" first, introduce "saw" only after "was" is solid)
- the / they / then / there (introduce "the" first; others only in simple-sentences tier)
- where / were (defer entirely to simple-sentences)

Note on overlap with CVC distractor pool: "can," "not," "is," and "it" already appear as CVC words in the current phonics tiers. This is an asset, not a conflict — Marian has phonics-grounded prior exposure that becomes the anchor for orthographic mapping.

**L2 note:** Several function words (the, a, for, of) have no Tagalog structural equivalent. Emma must supply meaning through sentence context, not translation. "The" should be introduced in a simple carrier sentence ("The cat."), not in isolation.

## Recognition mechanic — core question

The CVC picker mechanic does NOT transfer cleanly to sight words. The reasons:

1. **Picture pairing is unhelpful for non-picturable words.** "The," "was," "said," "for," "of," "go" — none of these can be grounded in a picture the way "cat" or "dog" can. E4 (Conners 2012) shows pictures actually slow word learning for non-picturable words. The picture-chip row has no role here.

2. **The silent-text "decoding beat" is conceptually mismatched.** The 1,500 ms silent window in the CVC mechanic lets Marian try to sound out the target before Emma reads it aloud. For CVC words, this is correct pedagogy — sounding out is the point. For sight words, the goal is **instant whole-word recognition** built on orthographic mapping, not sounding out letter-by-letter. A silent decoding beat for "was" will produce the non-word /wæs/ by GPC rules — confusing rather than helpful, because the /ɒ/ vowel in "was" is the irregular part.

3. **The target-word carrier is different.** In CVC mode, Emma says "Tap the [target]" and Marian selects a picture chip. For sight words, the carrier is Emma saying the target word and Marian selecting the **written word** from visually-distinct alternatives. The pictures disappear; the words themselves become the chips.

**Recommended mechanic — audio-first written-word matching:**

Phase 1 (gentle ramp, new word introduction, problems 1-3):
- Emma speaks the target word in a carrier sentence ("The cat is here. Find: the.")
- Marian sees 3 written-word chips: target + 2 visually-distinct foils (e.g., "the" vs "cat" vs "go").
- No picture support for the target. (A small scene illustration may accompany the carrier sentence for contextual comprehension — not for the word itself.)
- Emma's audio chip plays automatically; Marian taps the matching written word.

Phase 2 (trap, practicing words, problems 4-8):
- Emma speaks the target word alone ("Was.")
- Marian sees 3 visually-similar written-word chips: target + 2 visual-shape neighbours (was/saw/for, the/he/she, said/and/saw).
- This tests true whole-word discrimination, not just "pick the only word I recognise."

**Distractor model:**
- **Gentle distractors (problems 1-3):** Visually and categorically distinct from target. "the" vs "cat" vs "go" (different length, different starting letter, no overlap).
- **Trap distractors (problems 4-8):** Visual-shape neighbours and high-frequency confusables. Axes: reverse-letter pairs (was/saw), first-letter matches (he/she), common-ending matches (for/far, of/off), grapheme-length near-matches (the/he, said/and).

This distractor axis is **different from the CVC phonics axes** (rhyme, onset, vowel). The whole-word recognition axes are visual and structural — not phonics-pattern-derived.

## Dark-pattern / age-fit audit

**Never a red X — preserved.** The chip-tap mechanic with Emma's puzzled-tilt on wrong answers maps directly onto the sight-word version. No design change needed.

**Cognitive load for 8-year-olds:** 3 chips (not 4) is the right count for sight-word matching, matching the existing WordSong chip count. At 8, working memory supports 3 simultaneous options comfortably; 4 adds meaningful load for a new abstraction (written function words with no picture anchor). The current WordSong 3-chip count is appropriate; no change.

**No speed feedback** — consistent with the locked speed-feedback ruling in `skill-trees-and-content.md`. Correct is correct regardless of latency. This is particularly important here: L2 learners need more processing time for function words that have no L1 equivalent. Penalising latency on "the" for Marian would be developmentally inappropriate.

**Audio-first is essential for L2.** Marian needs to hear the English word before she reads it, not simultaneously or after. The carrier sentence approach (E7) provides semantic scaffolding that visual-only word matching cannot provide for a child with limited English receptive vocabulary.

**No streak shame, no performance pressure** — same policy as existing tiers. Sight-word fluency is sensitive for L2 learners; the Emma puzzled-tilt is the only wrong-answer signal.

**Session length:** Sight-word sessions sit inside the existing 8-problem WordSong frame. 2 new words + 6 review is the right split for a 10-15-minute session; more new words at once would overwhelm working memory and prevent the 10-15 exposures needed for consolidation (E2, E8).

## Risks / counter-evidence

1. **The orthographic-mapping evidence is strong for L1 English readers; the evidence specific to Tagalog-L1 L2-English 8-year-olds is thin.** E7 is the closest, but it uses Chinese-English and Hindi-English populations. The recommendation to anchor sight-word learning in phonics still holds (Tagalog and English share many consonant phonemes; E1's mechanism is cross-linguistic), but the dosage and carrier-sentence complexity recommendations are extrapolated from L1 research rather than directly evidenced for Marian's exact profile. I am reasoning from the nearest solid evidence, labeled as such.

2. **Picture pairing is genuinely dead for function words, but concrete picturable sight words (go, see, can, run) might benefit from a scene illustration in the carrier sentence.** The E4 evidence against picture pairing used word-picture direct pairing; a scene illustration contextualising the whole sentence is different. This distinction is worth holding onto for the spec — the spec should use a scene illustration for the carrier sentence (contextual grounding) while keeping the tap-the-word chip row picture-free.

3. **The 2 new words/session dosage is practitioner consensus (E6), not RCT-validated.** Marian's August 2026 deadline creates pressure to go faster. I recommend 2 new per session as the default ceiling, with Thomas able to raise it to 3 if Marian is demonstrably breezing through the gentle phase. Do not go to 4 new per session — the working-memory ceiling for an 8-year-old is real.

4. **Confusable-pair sequencing needs active management.** The Dolch list contains several near-minimal-pair traps (was/saw, of/off, the/they, where/were). The spec must encode the sequencing rule: never introduce both members of a confusable pair in the same session or in adjacent sessions. The distractor model above (using confusables as trap distractors) is only safe once the target word is secure across 3+ sessions.

3. **Literacy Leitner box — RECOMMENDED as a downstream design ticket.** Spaced retrieval (E8) is the strongest evidence-based mechanism for moving sight words into long-term memory, and the math Leitner box infrastructure already exists. File a separate ticket for literacy Leitner design. Do not block Wave 11 content on it — the rotating-review bank of 4-6 words per session is sufficient scaffolding for the first wave.

4. **Distractor design is pedagogy-gated.** Kyle's TARGET_PAIRINGS matrix for sight words must use visual-shape / confusable-pair axes (not phonics-rhyme axes). This note is the citation for that gate.

### For Kyle (design spec)

5. **No picture chips for the target word.** The word itself is the chip. Scene illustration for carrier sentence context is acceptable; picture-on-chip is not.

6. **Remove the silent-text decoding beat for sight-words content type.** The 1,500 ms silent window in WordSong is correct for CVC decoding tiers; it is wrong for sight-word recognition. Emma's audio should play immediately (or with at most a 200-300 ms lead-in beat — no longer). The wordSongContentType discriminant should gain a new value `'sight-word'` to gate this behaviour.

7. **Carrier sentence for gentle phase, bare word for trap phase.** Problems 1-3: Emma speaks the word in a short carrier sentence ("The cat. Find: the."). Problems 4-8: Emma speaks the target word alone. This mirrors the existing gentle/trap logic structurally while fitting the new mechanic.

8. **Confusable-pair distractor pairing must be encoded in TARGET_PAIRINGS for sight-word entries.** The key pairs: was/saw, the/he, he/she, said/and, for/far, of/off, go/no, do/to. These are the trap-tier chips. The gentle-tier chips should differ in visual length and initial letter from the target.

9. **Introduce "the" first, alone, before any other article or near-confusable.** "The" is the single highest-frequency English word; securing it before everything else is the correct sequencing call.

## Leitner recommendation summary

A **literacy Leitner box** is recommended as a downstream design, for the following reasons:
- Sight-word fluency requires 10-15+ exposures spread over time (E2, E8). A Leitner box operationalises this precisely.
- The math Leitner infrastructure (`progress.mathFactsLeitner`, `leitner.ts`) is already built and the pattern is established.
- The relevant design question — how to key literacy Leitner items (word string vs word+tier) and how to weight box levels against the 2-new-per-session cap — is a separate scope decision that does not belong in this note.

**Flag as SEPARATE downstream design; do not design it here.** The rotating 4-6 review words per session is the v1 mechanism.
