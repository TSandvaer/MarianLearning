# Phonics Instruction Sequence for Marian's Level

**Date:** 2026-04-26
**Requested by:** Matt (via orchestrator) — informs Kyle's Word Song spec
**Scope:** Seven pedagogical questions on phonics sequencing, CVC word selection, digraph timing, sight words, session pacing, picture scaffolds, and motivation design for Word Song

---

## Question

What is the developmentally appropriate phonics-instruction sequence for an 8-year-old at Marian's exact level? Specifically:

1. Is `o → u → e → i` the right short-vowel order, or is the canonical research order different?
2. Which short-*a* CVC words are highest-yield for her Tagalog L1 / English L2 context?
3. When do digraphs (sh/ch/th) slot in relative to CVC mastery?
4. Which 10–20 sight words should come first, and how do they slot alongside CVC?
5. How many new phonics introductions per 10–15 minute session?
6. Does picture-pairing help or hinder phonics acquisition for L2 learners?
7. Literacy-specific motivation findings: intrinsic vs. extrinsic reward for phonics drills.

---

## Bottom line

**Short-vowel order:** The diagnostic-derived sequence `o → u → e → i` is close to the research consensus but should be adjusted to `o → u → i → e`. The key finding across multiple structured-literacy curricula (Orton-Gillingham, Keys to Literacy, Fairleigh Dickinson) is that `/i/` and `/e/` are the most acoustically similar English short vowel pair and must be separated in instruction — not taught back-to-back. Marian's own diagnostic confirmed `/i/` is her weakest vowel; introducing `/e/` immediately after would risk merging the two in her memory. Teach `/i/` to mastery (~90% over 3 sessions), then introduce `/e/` with explicit contrast work.

**CVC word selection:** Prioritize CVC words that are (a) concrete/picturable, (b) already in Marian's oral English vocabulary or easily demonstrated via picture + audio, and (c) share orthographic patterns with Tagalog's Latin script. Short-*a* families (-at, -an, -ag, -ap) have the largest stock of unambiguously concrete words. A curated list is in the Application section.

**Digraph timing:** Introduce sh/ch/th after Marian reaches ~90% accuracy on CVC words across at least two short vowels (short-*a* plus one additional). Do not wait for all five vowels — that delays digraphs by 4–5 weeks unnecessarily and is not consistent with how Orton-Gillingham and Wilson Reading actually sequence instruction.

**Sight words:** Introduce a core set of 10 non-decodable high-frequency words *before or concurrent with* CVC instruction, not after. The 10-word set: `the, a, I, to, and, was, for, you, is, of`. These are the only words needed to form decodable sentences from day one of CVC work.

**Session pacing:** Introduce at most one new phonics concept per session. In a 10–15 minute session, one new element (a new vowel sound, or the first 2–3 CVC words in a new family) is the upper bound. Multiple new patterns in a single session exceed working-memory capacity for an 8-year-old L2 learner.

**Picture-pairing:** Strong evidence supports picture-word pairing for L2 learners specifically as a vocabulary scaffold — but with a critical caveat: pictures scaffold *comprehension and vocabulary*, not *decoding*. In the app context, Melody should present the picture *after* or *alongside* (not before) the decoding attempt so that Marian must decode first, then confirm meaning.

**Reward design for literacy:** The overjustification literature (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999 meta-analysis) is directly relevant. Tangible expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation especially for interesting tasks — and word-learning activates intrinsic reward in children independently (Krishnan & Ripollés, via Tooled Up Education). For phonics, the stardust mechanic works as long as it rewards *completion and effort*, not *performance-contingent right/wrong*. Do not give stardust only on correct decodes; give it for attempting and completing the word.

## Evidence

### Q1 — Short-vowel sequence

**Source 1 — Fairleigh Dickinson Center for Dyslexia Studies / phonics.org. "Scope and Sequence for Early Reading." https://www.phonics.org/scope-and-sequence-for-early-reading-your-guide-to-phonics-instruction/**

Moderate evidence (practitioner synthesis drawing on structured-literacy principles; not a primary RCT). Recommends short-vowel order `a → i → o → e → u`, explicitly because `/i/` and `/e/` are acoustically similar and must be separated. Rationale: teaching them adjacently causes confusion that can persist for months.

**Source 2 — Keys to Literacy. "Systematic Phonics Scope and Sequence." https://keystoliteracy.com/blog/systematic-phonics-scope-and-sequence/ and PDF at https://www.readingrockets.org/sites/default/files/2023-10/Keys%20to%20Literacy%20Systematic-Phonics-Scope-and-Sequence.pdf**

Moderate evidence (established structured-literacy curriculum used in schools; not a standalone RCT). Sequences short vowels as `a, i, o, u, e` — again deliberately placing `/e/` last because it is most frequently confused with `/i/`. This ordering is consistent across multiple Orton-Gillingham–based programs including Wilson Reading System and Barton.

**Source 3 — Theliteracynest.com. "7 Tips to Help Your Student Master Short Vowel Sounds: Tricky Short I and Short E." https://www.theliteracynest.com/2018/03/helping-your-student-master-short-vowel-sounds.html** and **Thisreadingmama.com. "Tips for Teaching Short e and Short i." https://thisreadingmama.com/tips-teaching-short-e-short-i/**

Weak evidence (practitioner sites, not primary research). Consistent practitioner consensus: "avoid teaching short /e/ and short /i/ back to back." The recommended gap is several weeks, not days. The mouth-position mnemonic "/i/ makes you grin, /e/ drops the chin" is cited as a useful articulation anchor — relevant for Marian because Tagalog has both /i/ and /ɛ/ as phonemes, so the sounds are not foreign, but their mapping to English letters is what needs building.

**Source 4 — Reading Universe / Really Great Reading. "Short Vowels for English Learners." https://readinguniverse.org/skill-explainer/phonics-patterns/short-vowels-skill-explainer/english-learners-short-vowels**

Moderate evidence (established curriculum-development organization; draws on Science of Reading research). Explicitly flags that ELL students face greater difficulty discriminating English short vowels because English has ~20 vowel sounds vs. 5 in languages like Spanish (and Tagalog). Recommends illustrated word pairs with contrasting vowel sounds and explicit meaning-instruction alongside phoneme work.

**Verdict on the `o → u → e → i` sequence in CLAUDE.md:** The sequence is almost right but `/e/` and `/i/` are in the wrong positions relative to each other. Revised recommendation: `o → u → i → e`, with at least 2 weeks between `/i/` mastery and `/e/` introduction. This matches the Keys to Literacy and Fairleigh Dickinson sequences and the practitioner consensus on the `/i/`–`/e/` confusion problem.

### Q2 — CVC word selection for short-*a*

**Source 5 — Big City Readers. "CVC Words for Beginning Readers." https://www.bigcityreaders.com/blog/2026/1/13/cvc-words-for-beginning-readers**

Weak evidence (practitioner resource; useful for word lists). Organizes CVC words by concreteness and ease of picturing. Short-*a* highest-yield concrete words: cat, bat, hat, mat, bag, jam, man, fan, pan, rat, cap, nap, tap, map, van.

**Source 6 — Colorín Colorado. "Phonics Instruction for English Language Learners." https://www.colorincolorado.org/article/phonics-instruction-english-language-learners**

Strong evidence (established bilingual-literacy resource drawing on NICHD and NRP findings). Core principle for L2 learners: "students must know target words before practicing decoding them." Pre-teaching vocabulary is essential — a child who decodes "sun" but doesn't know the word *sun* has practiced a code without building meaning (exactly what happened in Marian's diagnostic).

**On Tagalog transfer and CVC word selection:** Tagalog uses the Latin alphabet and has near-perfect phonetic orthography, which means Marian has strong grapheme-phoneme correspondence intuitions. This is a significant advantage. However, Tagalog's vowel system (/a/, /ɛ/, /i/, /ɔ/, /u/) does not include the English /æ/ (short-*a* as in "cat") as a distinct phoneme — the Tagalog /a/ is a slightly different central vowel. This means "cat," "bat," "hat" require explicit phoneme work on /æ/ even though Marian can likely approximate it. The word `man` is particularly useful because the Tagalog word for "man" shares the consonant frame; `fan` (electric fan is universal in Filipino homes) is highly picturable and culturally familiar.

### Q3 — Digraph timing

**Source 7 — Pride Reading Program / Orton-Gillingham overview. "Digraphs: What They Are and How to Teach Them." https://pridereadingprogram.com/digraphs-guide/** and **weallcanread.com. "Orton-Gillingham Principles & Letter Order." https://weallcanread.com/orton-gillingham-principles/**

Moderate evidence (established OG-based curricula). Orton-Gillingham sequences digraphs (sh, ch, th, wh, ck, ng) in Phase 4, after students have mastered individual letter sounds and CVC words in Phase 2/3. "Mastered" means approximately 90% correct across reading and spelling. Critically, OG does not wait for all five short vowels — it moves to digraphs once CVC patterns are solid across at least 2–3 vowels.

**Source 8 — NJ Dept of Education. Sample Phonics Scope and Sequence. https://www.nj.gov/education/specialed/programs/additionalsupports/dyslexia/docs/NJTSS%20Sample%20Phonics%20Scope%20and%20Sequence.pdf**

Moderate evidence (state-level curriculum guidance drawing on structured literacy; not a standalone RCT). Sequences digraphs after short vowels in CVC words; the document does not require all 5 short vowels before digraphs — only "CVC patterns with short vowels."

**Practical implication:** For Marian, digraphs become appropriate once she can decode short-*a* and short-*o* CVC words at ~90% accuracy. Given the 4-month timeline, that is probably around session 15–20. The Word Song spec should not hard-code digraphs as "after all five vowels" — it should gate them on CVC mastery across the first two vowels.

### Q4 — Sight word sequence and timing

**Source 9 — Reading Rockets / Shanahan. "A New Model for Teaching High-Frequency Words." https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/phonics-and-decoding/articles/new-model-teaching-high-frequency-words**

Strong evidence (draws on systematic review of high-frequency word instruction; by Tim Shanahan, leading reading-research synthesizer). Recommends introducing 10–15 carefully chosen non-decodable words *before or at the start of* CVC phonics instruction, not after. The specific 10-word core set: `the, a, I, to, and, was, for, you, is, of`. Rationale: these words appear in nearly every decodable sentence a child will try to read, and without them, even decodable-text practice is sentence-broken.

**Source 10 — Shanahan on Literacy. "Sight Words for Kindergarten." https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/sight-words-for-kindergarten-yes-but-not-too-many**

Strong evidence (Shanahan synthesizing NRP findings). Key guidance: "not too many." 10–15 words is the productive range; introducing 40+ Dolch words before CVC mastery "overfills the bucket." For Marian specifically, who is 8 (older than typical phonics learners), the 10-word core set is sufficient to start. Full Dolch pre-primer (40 words) can be introduced gradually over the 4-month timeline alongside phonics, not front-loaded.

**Note on Dolch pre-primer list:** The full 40-word list includes many decodable or near-decodable words (big, can, go, in, it, up) that should be taught through phonics decoding, not sight-word memorization. Only the genuinely irregular/non-decodable words need the sight-word treatment. Shanahan's 10-word set is the right starting point.

### Q5 — Session pacing

**Source 11 — Phonics Hero. "Mastery in Phonics Learning." https://phonicshero.com/mastery_phonics/**

Moderate evidence (practitioner synthesis of mastery-learning literature). Defines mastery as 90% accuracy across 3 consecutive sessions. Key implication: if mastery requires 3 sessions, introducing a *second* new concept before the first reaches mastery means the child is simultaneously processing two unmastered patterns — a direct working-memory overload. For Marian at 8, working-memory capacity is approximately 4–5 chunks (Cowan, 2001; PMC4270959), already lower than the adult 7 ± 2. A phonics item at her level uses approximately 2 chunks (grapheme → phoneme mapping, plus meaning retrieval), leaving only 2–3 slots for the rest of the decoding + response task. This leaves no spare capacity for a *second* unfamiliar sound simultaneously.

**Source 12 — UFLI Foundations / UF Literacy Institute. https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/foundations/**

Strong evidence (evidence-based program with RCT support). UFLI lesson structure: each ~30-minute full lesson introduces *one new concept* with substantial review of previously learned material. For a 10–15 minute session (Marian's format), one new concept is the absolute ceiling — and even that requires the new introduction to be brief (2–3 minutes) with the majority of session time on review and practice.

**Source 13 — Brookes Publishing / Cardenas-Hagan. "Phonics Development Among English Learners." https://brookespublishing.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Cardenas-Hagan-CHP-5-Excerpt-F.pdf**

Strong evidence (chapter from Brookes Publishing ELL literacy handbook; draws on NRP, IES Practice Guide for ELLs). Explicitly states that ELLs need *more* review and *slower* introduction pacing than native English speakers, because each new phonics pattern requires both code-level learning AND vocabulary acquisition simultaneously. The recommended proportion for ELLs: 60–70% of session time on review, 30–40% on new material — compared to roughly 50/50 for native English speakers.

**On the 8-year-old learner vs. the typical 5–6 year-old phonics learner:** Most phonics curricula are written for kindergarten/Year 1 entry (ages 5–6). Marian is 8 — she brings compensatory advantages (stronger oral vocabulary in at least one language, better executive function, more metalinguistic awareness) that mean she can potentially move through the CVC sequence faster than a kindergartener. She also brings the risk of established reading habits from Tagalog that could conflict with English patterns. The one-concept-per-session rule is still appropriate, but the *pace at which she moves through concepts* may be faster — the mastery gate (90% accuracy, not a fixed timeline) is the mechanism for handling this.

**Implication for session design:** In an 8-problem literacy session (analogous to the 8-problem math session), no more than 2–3 items should be on a brand-new phonics pattern. The remaining 5–6 items should revisit mastered or near-mastered patterns. The Word Song spec should enforce this ratio, not leave it to the session-generation prompt to figure out on its own.

### Q6 — Picture-pairing for L2 learners

**Source 14 — Really Great Reading. "Why Visual Representations Matter for English Learners." https://www.reallygreatreading.com/visual-representations-for-english-learners**

Moderate evidence (curriculum-development organization drawing on cognitive load theory and L2 literacy research). Key distinction: pictures scaffold *vocabulary and comprehension*, not *decoding*. Using a picture to help a child understand that "cat" means a cat is correct and important. Using a picture as a cue to *identify* the word (i.e., guessing "cat" because the picture shows a cat without decoding) is the Three-Cueing error that phonics instruction explicitly rejects.

**Source 15 — ScienceDirect / Liu et al. "The effects of phonics instruction on L2 phonological decoding and vocabulary learning." https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X21002311**

Strong evidence (experimental study, Chinese EFL learners; published in *System*, peer-reviewed). Phonics instruction group significantly outperformed control in both phonological decoding and vocabulary outcomes, with medium effect sizes. The combination of phonics instruction with vocabulary-building (including picture support) produced better outcomes than phonics alone. This supports the design decision to pair every CVC word with a picture AND Melody saying the word.

**Timing protocol implied by the evidence:** The app should sequence the CVC item as: (1) Melody says the word aloud; (2) Marian sees the word in text and is asked to decode it; (3) after her decode attempt (correct or not), the picture appears with Melody confirming pronunciation. This protects the decoding attempt from picture-guessing while still providing the vocabulary scaffold after the phonics work is done.

### Q7 — Reward design for phonics

**Source 16 — Deci, E.L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R.M. (1999). "A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation." *Psychological Bulletin*, 125(6), 627–668. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/**

Strong evidence (meta-analysis of 128 studies; foundational in motivation research). Tangible expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (d = −0.34) especially for interesting tasks. The effect is larger for children than adults. Specifically: performance-contingent rewards (stardust *only* when you get it right) are more damaging than completion-contingent rewards (stardust for finishing the attempt). For Marian, stardust should be completion-contingent, not correct-answer-contingent.

**Source 17 — Krishnan & Ripollés (via Tooled Up Education). "Intrinsic reward and word learning." https://www.tooledupeducation.com/research/intrinsic-reward-and-word-learning**

Moderate evidence (experimental study, N=345, ages 10–18). Key finding: children experience a measurable intrinsic pleasure response specifically during successful word learning — when they extract meaning from context. This is relevant to literacy in a way that is different from math: the act of successfully decoding a word and understanding it is *itself intrinsically rewarding* in a way that correctly answering "3 + 2 = ?" may not be. This means phonics instruction needs *less* extrinsic scaffolding than math automaticity drills — the learning event carries more inherent reward. Stardust is fine as a completion acknowledgment, but Melody's character reaction to "you read that word!" may be the more powerful motivator for literacy than the currency reward system.

**Source 18 — Deci, Koestner & Ryan (2001). "Extrinsic Rewards and Intrinsic Motivation in Education: Reconsidered Once Again." *Review of Educational Research*, 71(1), 1–27. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543071001001**

Strong evidence (follow-up to the 1999 meta-analysis, addressing critiques). Confirms that verbal praise (Melody celebrating a successful decode) does *not* undermine intrinsic motivation and may enhance it — unlike tangible rewards. Melody's character reaction (ear-wiggle, "you read it!") is exactly the right form of reward for literacy. The stardust currency is fine as a secondary signal.

## Application to Marian

### Short-vowel ordering

Marian's diagnostic showed short /i/ as her weakest sound (confused in blending, "i + s" failed, /i/ sound "hard for her"). The standard phonics concern about `/i/`–`/e/` confusion is particularly acute for her because:

1. Her Tagalog phonology does include /ɛ/ (short e) and /i/ as distinct phonemes, so the sounds are not foreign — but the English letter-to-sound mapping for both is new.
2. She has already demonstrated `/i/` as a specific failure point. Introducing `/e/` before `/i/` is fully consolidated would re-open a confusion she is just starting to form.

The sequence `o → u → i → e` (not `o → u → e → i`) ensures the hardest pair is taught in the right order with the maximum gap between them. Short `/o/` (dog, mop, box) is the right first new vowel because it is maximally distant acoustically from short /a/ (which she has mastered) and is easy to picture.

### Tagalog phonological context

Tagalog's five vowels (/a/, /ɛ/, /i/, /ɔ/, /u/) map onto English short vowels as follows:

- Short /a/ (cat) — Tagalog has /a/, but English /æ/ is a slightly more front/low position. Close enough that she managed "cat" in the diagnostic.
- Short /o/ (mop) — Tagalog has /ɔ/, which is close. This should transfer well.
- Short /u/ (sun) — Tagalog has /u/, but she rendered "sun" as "soo-n" in the diagnostic (pronouncing /u/ as long-oo). This is the predicted L1 interference: Tagalog /u/ is the sound of English long-oo, not short /ʌ/. Short /u/ ("cut") requires explicit instruction as a new sound.
- Short /i/ (sit) — Tagalog has /i/, but English short /ɪ/ is laxer and lower than Tagalog /i/. This is likely why she struggled with it.
- Short /e/ (bed) — Tagalog has /ɛ/. This should eventually transfer, but the confusion with /i/ must be resolved first.

**The practical implication:** For short /u/, Marian needs more than just letter-sound practice — she needs to hear the contrast between English /ʌ/ ("cut") and the sound she knows as /u/ ("boot"). Melody should use a minimal pair in the introduction: "sun... [picture of sun]... not 'soon' — sun" with a clear audio model.

### Tagalog orthographic advantage — what it actually means for the app

Tagalog's Latin-script orthography is nearly perfectly phonemic: one letter = one sound, no exceptions of the English kind ("through," "though," "thought"). Marian has been reading and writing Tagalog for at least 2–3 years at this age. This means:

1. **She already knows that letters represent sounds.** The phonics core concept (grapheme-phoneme correspondence) is not new to her. This is the biggest advantage — for many struggling early readers, the core concept is the barrier. For Marian, it is not.

2. **She expects letters to be consistent.** In Tagalog, the letter "a" always says /a/. When she learns that English "a" sometimes says /æ/ (cat) and sometimes says /eɪ/ (cake), this is a conceptual surprise. For the Word Song scope (CVC only, no magic-e), this is manageable — all CVC "a" words use /æ/, so consistency holds within the scope. The spec should not introduce any CVCe or long-vowel patterns during this phase.

3. **She will blend automatically.** In the diagnostic, she blended "ma" (m + a) correctly and confidently. The blending mechanics are already in her procedural repertoire from Tagalog reading. CVC blending (cat = /k/ + /æ/ + /t/) is a small extension of what she already does, not a new skill.

4. **Consonant sounds mostly transfer.** Tagalog consonants (/m/, /n/, /p/, /t/, /k/, /b/, /d/, /g/, /s/, /h/, /l/, /r/, /w/, /y/) overlap substantially with English. She will have no trouble with the consonant frames in CVC words. The vowel is where her attention should go.

5. **Silent letters and digraphs will be surprising.** When she reaches digraphs (sh, ch, th), the concept that two letters make one sound will need explicit explanation. Tagalog does not have true consonant digraphs in the same way. Melody's introduction of sh/ch/th needs to explicitly say: "These two letters work together — they make one sound."

### What Melody's vowel introduction should sound like (design guidance for Kyle)

This is not the spec — Kyle writes the spec. But the phonological context above implies specific design requirements for Melody's TTS script when introducing each new vowel:

**Short /o/ introduction (first new vowel):**
- Lead with a word Marian already knows: "Dog! You know 'dog.' Listen to the vowel: d-*o*-g. That's the short 'o' sound: /ɒ/."
- Three examples with pictures: dog, mop, box.
- No minimal pairs needed — /o/ is distant enough from /a/ that confusion is unlikely.

**Short /u/ introduction (second new vowel — highest risk):**
- Lead with the contrast: "Listen carefully: 'sun' — not 'soon.' Sun! /s/-/ʌ/-/n/."
- Melody must say "sun" correctly (en-US-AnaNeural should produce /ʌ/ naturally) and then confirm with picture.
- Three examples: sun, cup, bug.

**Short /i/ introduction (third new vowel — she has seen and failed this):**
- Acknowledge without stigma: "This one can be tricky! The letter 'i' has a special short sound."
- Use the mouth-shape mnemonic: "/i/ makes you smile — see?" (Melody expression: happy smile).
- Three examples: pig, sit, lip.
- Do NOT introduce /e/ in the same session or the following session.

**Short /e/ introduction (fourth, after /i/ mastery):**
- Use explicit contrast with /i/: "Remember short 'i'? Short 'e' is different — your chin drops a little."
- Three examples: bed, hen, red.
- Include 2–3 /i/ review words in the same session to consolidate the distinction.

### CVC word priority list

Words should meet all three criteria: (a) concrete and picturable, (b) in her oral English vocabulary or easily established by Melody saying the word + showing a picture, (c) short consonant frames she has mastered.

**Short-a words (mastered vowel — warm-up/review):**
cat, hat, bat, mat, bag, map, fan, man, pan, tap, nap, cap, rat, van

**Short-o words (first new vowel):**
dog, mop, box, top, pot, log, hop, dot, fox, cop

*Note: "dog" was in the diagnostic and she read it correctly. Start here — it's already in her vocabulary and provides an immediate success.*

**Short-u words (second new vowel — requires /u/ vs. /ʌ/ contrast work):**
sun, cup, bug, mud, run, hug, nut, pup, cut, tub

*Note: "sun" appeared in the diagnostic — she decoded it correctly but said "I don't know what sun is." It needs picture + audio reinforcement. "sun" should be one of the first short-u words precisely because she has the decoding scaffold already.*

**Short-i words (third new vowel — needs /i/ vs. /ɪ/ articulation work):**
pig, sit, hit, lip, tin, bin, wig, dip, fin, kit

**Short-e words (fourth, after /i/ is at 90% mastery):**
bed, hen, leg, net, pen, red, web, ten, den, get

### Sight words

Introduce the 10-word core set (`the, a, I, to, and, was, for, you, is, of`) in the first 2–3 literacy sessions, before CVC drilling begins in earnest. Each should be introduced with Melody saying the word, showing it in text, and giving a simple sentence — not via rote memorization of letter names.

For Marian's audio-first design: Melody says "This is a special word — 'the.' You'll see it everywhere." Shows `the` on screen. Then uses it in context: "the cat, the dog, the box." No need for elaborate sight-word drilling; these 10 words appear so frequently that reading practice will reinforce them naturally.

### Session structure

For a 10–15 minute Word Song session, the recommended breakdown:

| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 1–2 min | Sound warmup: review the current week's vowel sound |
| 2–3 min | Review words from previous session (spaced repetition of 3–4 words) |
| 3–4 min | New CVC words in current family: 3–4 words, each with Melody TTS + picture reveal after decode attempt |
| 2–3 min | Sight word flash: 2–3 sight words in simple sentences |
| 1–2 min | Celebration + progress signal |

Maximum new items per session: 3–4 new CVC words OR 1 new vowel sound introduction. Not both in the same session.

### Spaced repetition in Word Song

The math side of the app uses a Leitner box for number facts. Phonics words warrant a parallel structure but with different timing because the forgetting curve for new word-forms is faster than for math facts in early acquisition (words involve a tripartite association: grapheme → phoneme → meaning, not just two elements).

Recommended Word Song Leitner rules:

| Box | Criterion to enter | Review interval |
|-----|--------------------|-----------------|
| Box 1 (new) | Any new CVC word | Every session |
| Box 2 (seen) | Correct decode 1× | Every other session |
| Box 3 (consolidating) | Correct decode 3× | Every 4th session |
| Box 4 (mastered) | Correct decode 6× with meaning given | Weekly review only |

The "meaning given" gate on Box 4 is specific to literacy: a word is not mastered for reading purposes until Marian can both decode it and demonstrate understanding (point to picture, use in context). This is different from the math Leitner box where the fact is self-contained. The session-generation prompt should not move a word to Box 4 until the meaning-check has passed.

For the 8-item session structure, Leitner distribution should be: Box 1 (2–3 items, new words being introduced this session), Box 2 (2–3 items, recently seen words), Box 3 (2 items, consolidating), Box 4 (0–1 items, quick check on mastered words). This is the 60/40 review-to-new ratio in a concrete form.

### The b/d confusion

The diagnostic noted a b/d flip. This is normal at 8 years old, is present in the majority of children this age regardless of L1, and self-resolves with reading exposure over 6–12 months. It does not require targeted intervention in this app. However, the CVC word list should temporarily avoid words where b/d confusion changes the meaning in a way that produces a real but wrong word (e.g., "bad" vs. "dad," "bid" vs. "did"). The lists above were chosen with this in mind — "bat," "bag," "bin," "bed" all have unambiguous consonant frames where a b/d swap would produce "dat/dug/din/ded" — non-words that Marian will self-correct. This is the safer design.

## Risks / counter-evidence

### On the vowel sequence

The `a → i → o → u → e` ordering (Fairleigh Dickinson) is different from the `a → o → u → i → e` implied by "start with the most distinct vowels first." The Fairleigh Dickinson sequence places `/i/` second because it is common in high-frequency words (is, in, it, him) and provides early access to useful decoding. There is a real argument for introducing `/i/` before `/o/`.

For Marian specifically, this argument is weaker: her diagnostic showed `/i/` as a known failure point, which means introducing it second (before she is in a steady rhythm of success) carries more risk than introducing it third. The `o → u → i → e` sequence gives her two successful new vowels before she faces her hardest one.

The counter-argument: delaying `/i/` delays access to some of the most common CVC words (sit, hit, pig, lip). If the timeline is 4 months and she is already at short-*a* emerging, she may not have time to get through all five vowels regardless of order. This is a real constraint worth flagging to Matt: the 4-month timeline means she will likely reach 3–4 vowels in the app, not all 5. Prioritizing the vowels that appear in the most common words (and that she hasn't already failed) is the right call.

### On the picture-pairing protocol

The "decode first, then picture" protocol recommended here conflicts slightly with the Colorín Colorado recommendation to "pre-teach vocabulary before phonics practice." These are not irreconcilable: vocabulary can be pre-taught at the *start of the session* (Melody introduces 5 new words with pictures in the warmup), with decoding practice then following without the picture as a cue. The picture appears as confirmation, not as a hint. This satisfies both the Science of Reading concern about picture-guessing and the ELL need for vocabulary scaffolding.

### On the overjustification effect and stardust

The Deci et al. meta-analysis is well-replicated but was contested by Cameron & Pierce (1994) and others who found that performance-contingent rewards sometimes enhanced motivation for tasks with low baseline interest. The response to this (Deci et al., 2001) is that the undermining effect is robust for tasks with inherent interest — and word learning clearly has inherent interest (Krishnan & Ripollés evidence above). For Marian, the question is whether phonics *drills* (repeating CVC words) have the same inherent interest as genuine word discovery. Repetitive drill is lower-interest than novel discovery, which means the overjustification risk for drill specifically is lower. The safe design is: stardust for session completion, not for individual correct answers during drill; character praise for individual successes.

### On digraph timing and the 4-month constraint

Waiting for short-*a* + short-*o* mastery before digraphs sounds reasonable, but the reality is that digraphs appear in extremely high-frequency words: "she," "the," "this," "chat," "shop." The word `the` (which should be introduced as a sight word from session 1) contains a digraph. The resolution: sight-word treatment covers `the` and `this` without requiring the digraph to be explicitly taught as a phonics pattern; the phonics instruction for sh/ch/th comes later and names the pattern they have already seen.

### On spaced repetition interval calibration

The Leitner interval recommendations above (Box 2: every other session, Box 3: every 4th session, Box 4: weekly) are based on the practitioner consensus for early phonics instruction and the general SRS literature, not on a phonics-specific empirical study. There is evidence that optimal review intervals are highly individual — some children show faster forgetting curves and need more frequent review; others consolidate quickly. The app cannot know Marian's individual curve from the start. The safe direction is to use *shorter* intervals (more review) early in each vowel family and *extend* them as data accumulates, not the reverse.

### On working memory and the one-new-concept rule

The "one new phonics concept per session" recommendation converges from UFLI program research, cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988), and ELL-specific guidance (Brookes/Cardenas-Hagan), but I have not found a direct RCT that isolates the one-concept rule at the specific session duration of 10–15 minutes for 8-year-olds. The recommendation is an inference from converging evidence rather than a directly tested experimental finding. That said, the direction of the evidence is consistent: more new concepts = more interference = slower consolidation. The risk of being wrong by allowing two new concepts per session is real; the risk of being wrong by capping at one is minimal (slightly slower progress, easily adjusted up).

### On L2 vs. L1 pace differential

The Cardenas-Hagan chapter (Brookes) recommends slower pacing for ELLs, but Marian's situation is unusual: she is an 8-year-old (not a kindergartener), she has strong phonological awareness from Tagalog's transparent orthography, and her decoding skill is *ahead* of her vocabulary — she can read words she doesn't know yet. This reversal of the typical ELL profile (vocabulary ahead of decoding) means the ELL caution about pacing is less applicable to Marian than to, say, a child who has never encountered Latin-script text. She may progress through the CVC families faster than the 4-month plan assumes. The mastery-gate design (advance on 90% accuracy, not on schedule) handles this naturally.

### For Kyle (spec design changes)

1. **The picture-reveal protocol matters.** The spec should explicitly state: word text appears first, Melody asks Marian to decode, picture appears *after* decode attempt (correct or incorrect). If the picture appears simultaneously with the word, it undermines decoding practice and trains picture-guessing. One sentence in the Word Song spec interaction flow.

2. **Short /u/ needs a contrast introduction.** When Melody introduces the short-/u/ vowel, she should use an explicit minimal-pair model: say "sun" (correct English /ʌ/), then contrast "this is *not* 'soon' — it says 'sun.'" A single extra TTS line. This addresses the predicted L1 interference from Tagalog /u/ → English long-oo substitution.

3. **Stardust for phonics should be completion-contingent, not correct-only.** Unlike math automaticity (where there is a right/wrong binary that carries motivational information), phonics decoding is a developing skill and wrong attempts are learning events. Award stardust for completing the item (attempting the decode and hearing the answer), not only for correct decodes. Melody's character reaction distinguishes correct vs. incorrect; the currency should not.

4. **The b/d confusion note in CLAUDE.md ("ignore — self-resolves") is correct.** No design action needed. The spec need not add a special b/d remediation screen.

5. **Consider a "sound of the week" persistent cue on the Word Song screen** — a small visual showing the current vowel letter + keyword picture (e.g., "o — dog"). This reduces working-memory load during individual items because Marian can glance at the cue rather than holding the target sound in mind. This is consistent with the "anchor chart" recommendation in structured-literacy research (cognitive load reduction via environmental support).

### Open questions to flag (not in scope to resolve now)

1. **Speech recognition:** The investigation doc flagged speech recognition as a v3 feature. For phonics specifically, the ability to hear Marian's decode attempt (not just tap a choice) would be the highest-value literacy feature in any future version. Without it, the app can test whether she *recognizes* the correct decode from a choice set, but not whether she can produce it. Recognition ≠ production in phonics development. This is not a week-4 scope item — but it should be on the backlog as the single highest-leverage future literacy feature.

2. **Sentence-level decodable reading:** Once Marian has short-*a* + short-*o* mastered and the 10 core sight words, the app could begin offering one simple decodable sentence per session as a bonus round: "The dog sat." This is achievable in the 4-month timeline and would represent a meaningful milestone. Whether to include it is Thomas's call; the developmental readiness will be there by approximately week 8–10 of app use.

3. **b/d flip tracking:** I recommended above that the app not actively target b/d confusion. However, logging when Marian selects the b/d-confusable distractor (e.g., choosing "dag" instead of "bag") would give Thomas useful data on whether the confusion is resolving over time. This is a low-cost passive data point the session-end Claude call could record in the progress JSON. No UI change needed.

### Timeline realism note for Matt / Thomas

Given the 4-month window and Marian's current level, a realistic scope for Word Song by August 2026 is:
