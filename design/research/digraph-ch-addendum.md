## Evidence

### §1 — /tʃ/ articulation for Marian (Tagalog L1, 8 years old)

#### §1a — Tagalog's affricate inventory and L1 transfer for /tʃ/

**Source 1 — Wikipedia. "Tagalog phonology." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagalog_phonology**

Informational (descriptive linguistics; not primary acquisition research). Standard Tagalog's native consonant inventory (16 phonemes: /p, t, k, ʔ, b, d, g, m, n, ŋ, s, h, l, ɾ, w, j/) does NOT include /tʃ/ or /ʃ/ as native phonemes. However, unlike /ʃ/, the affricate /tʃ/ entered Tagalog through Spanish and English loanwords and has achieved the status of a marginal phoneme in the loanword layer. Words like `tsaa` (/tʃaʔa/, 'tea') and English loanwords such as `church`, `cheese`, and `chips` carry /tʃ/ in educated and mesolectal Filipino speech.

This is the key asymmetry with sh: /tʃ/ has a foothold in Tagalog-via-English loanwords; /ʃ/ does not. Marian's home language environment in the Philippines will have exposed her to /tʃ/ in high-frequency English loanwords in ways /ʃ/ simply is not present.

**Source 2 — Kollective Hustle. "The Origin of the Filipino Accent." https://www.kollectivehustle.com/blog/the-origin-of-the-filipino-accent**

Weak evidence (practitioner synthesis of Filipino phonology; not primary research). Documents the pattern: Tagalog speakers who have acquired English loanwords with /tʃ/ typically retain the affricate in acrolectal and mesolectal speech. The /tʃ/ phoneme is produced more reliably by Filipino English speakers than /ʃ/ precisely because its entry into Tagalog via loanwords is older and more established.

**Source 3 — Phonological Features of Basilectal Philippine English. Academic Journals / DLSU. https://www.dlsu.edu.ph/wp-content/uploads/pdf/conferences/research-congress-proceedings/2016/LLI/LLI-II-02.pdf**

Moderate evidence (descriptive study of Philippine English sociolects; DLSU conference paper). At the basilect level, /tʃ/ is sometimes realized as /ts/ word-initially (a partial affricate that substitutes the post-alveolar /tʃ/ with a dental/alveolar release). At the acrolect/mesolect, /tʃ/ is typically produced as in American English. The study confirms the sociolectal gradient: more educated/formal-register speakers produce /tʃ/ cleanly; basilectal speakers produce /ts/ or /s/ approximations.

**Application to Marian:** At 8 years old in a Filipino home where English is used moderately (sufficient to establish some literacy; the Tagalog L1 is primary), Marian is most likely at the mesolect-adjacent register for familiar English loanwords. "Church," "cheese," and "chips" are all common in Filipino-English daily speech. She has likely heard these words with a recognizable /tʃ/ onset. Her perception of /tʃ/ is therefore better-calibrated than her perception of /ʃ/ was when sh was introduced. Production is the variable: she may produce /ts/ rather than the pure English /tʃ/, which is fine in the chip-tap format (recognition task, not production).

**Source 4 — Cambridge Core. "Philippine English (Metro Manila acrolect)." Journal of the International Phonetic Association. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-international-phonetic-association/article/philippine-english-metro-manila-acrolect/F041BD5DCF9DC4B66F822D30F784EF82**

Moderate evidence (academic phonetic description; peer-reviewed IPA journal). Confirms that the acrolectal Manila English variety preserves /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ as in American English. The implication: for a child in a household with any exposure to educated Filipino English (media, school, family), the /tʃ/ → /ts/ substitution is a basilect feature she may produce but probably also recognizes as differing from the target.

#### §1b — Ch vs. sh discrimination risk

**Source 5 — Primed for Primary. "Minimal Pairs SH and CH: How to Get Students to Hear the Difference." https://primedforprimary.com/minimal-pairs-sh-and-ch/**

Moderate evidence (structured-literacy practitioner resource with explicit phonetic grounding). Documents that sh/ch confusion is common in early readers across L1 backgrounds because the two sounds share articulatory features: both are palatal fricative-adjacent, both are produced with similar lip rounding and similar tongue body position. The key distinguishing feature is that /tʃ/ has a stop onset (the tongue tip briefly blocks airflow before releasing) that /ʃ/ lacks. This means:

- /tʃ/ cannot be held continuously (you cannot say "ch...ch...ch..." and sustain the sound); /ʃ/ can ("shhhh...")
- The stop release in /tʃ/ is audible as a brief pop before the friction
- Visual cue: the articulatory distinction is not visible from the outside (lips and jaw position are similar)

The diagnostic minimal pairs for sh/ch discrimination: `chip` vs. `ship`, `chop` vs. `shop`, `chat` vs. `shat` (avoid in class), `chest` vs. `shest` (non-word, useful for discrimination drills). The pairs `chip/ship` and `chop/shop` are high-value because both words in each pair are known, picturable, and already in Marian's sh-tier pool.

For Marian specifically: she has already been introduced to `ship` and `shop` in the sh tier. Introducing `chip` and `chop` in the ch tier creates natural discrimination anchor pairs. This is the correct pedagogical use of sequencing sh before ch — the sh words become the discrimination foil set.

**Source 6 — ELLII (formerly ESL Library). "A Fun Way to Introduce /ch/ and /sh/ to Kids." https://ellii.com/blog/ch-and-sh**

Weak evidence (ESL practitioner resource). Recommends explicit side-by-side comparison of ch and sh sounds as soon as ch is introduced, using the "can you hold the sound?" test (sh: yes; ch: no). The contrast cue leverages a physical, non-articulatory signal that an 8-year-old can self-test. This is accessible for Marian regardless of her Tagalog phonology because it is a duration test, not a phonetic production judgment.

#### §1c — The c-says-k orthographic confusion risk

This is the ch digraph's distinctive complication compared to sh. Marian already knows that the letter `c` says /k/ (from words like `cat`, `cap`, `cup`). When she encounters `ch`, the default hypothesis from her existing c-phoneme knowledge is: "c = /k/, h = /h/, so ch = /kh/ or /k/." This is not a production error — it is a grapheme-mapping hypothesis that the letter sequence might generate.

The confusion is documented in structured-literacy practice:

**Source 7 — The Reading Advice Hub. "Ch Digraph Words and Sounds." https://thereadingadvicehub.com/ch-digraph-words-and-sounds/**

Moderate evidence (practitioner resource with Science of Reading grounding). Notes explicitly: "children get confused with CH because... many elementary kids struggle to hear the difference between sounds associated with this grapheme." The three sounds the `ch` grapheme represents in English are: /tʃ/ (chin, chip — the target phoneme), /k/ (school, Christmas, anchor — the orthographic trap), and /ʃ/ (chef, champagne — French loanwords, not in scope). The /k/ realization of `ch` (as in "Christmas," "school," "chorus") is the orthographic confusion risk for a child who is pattern-matching from her existing phoneme knowledge.

The app's word pool mitigates this automatically: all recommended ch-tier words use the /tʃ/ realization only. The risk materializes only if the pool or Emma's script includes example words where `c` appears in other positions within the same session (e.g., a session that mixes ch-words with c-words like `cat` or `cup` as distractors). Kyle's distractor design should ensure that distractors for ch-words do not include c-initial words where `c` = /k/, to avoid the within-session orthographic signal: "sometimes c-words say /k/ but this c-word says /tʃ/."

Emma's introduction script should explicitly name this: "When `c` and `h` work together, they make the special `ch` sound — not the /k/ sound! `ch` says..." This is the ch-specific metalinguistic move that sh did not need (sh does not have a competing grapheme-phoneme mapping from prior instruction).

#### §2b — Word position: initial vs. final ch

The sh tier delivered three sh-final words alongside four sh-initial words (ship/shell/shed/shop initial; shoe/sheep/shark hybrid). For the ch tier, the word-final position is available with clean short-vowel patterns: `rich` (short-i CVCC), `much` (short-u CVCC).

However, CVCC patterns (consonant-vowel-consonant-consonant) are a step beyond pure CVC. For the ch tier in the app's chip-tap recognition format, this is manageable: the digraph is the same `ch` regardless of position. The decoding is not requested (recognition only), so the CVCC complexity matters less than it would in a spelling or production task.

**Pedagogical note:** Introduce word-initial ch words first (chin, chip, chop, chat, chest). Add word-final ch words (rich, much) as secondary targets once initial-position ch is established. The reading-direction argument (left-to-right decoding flow) applies here as it did for sh: the digraph at word-onset is encountered first and is more predictable in the decoding arc.

#### §3b — much: recommend replacement

`much` has two problems that compound:

1. **Unpicturable**: "much" is a function word / degree adverb. It modifies quantities and cannot be depicted as a standalone object. Even contextual pictures (a large pile of objects) read as "a lot" or "many" rather than "much." The picture scaffold fails.

2. **Grammatical word class**: function words in chip-tap recognition require hearing the word in context, not just hearing it in isolation. Emma saying "much" in isolation is semantically strange to an 8-year-old. Compare to `shoe`, `ship`, `chin` — nouns and concrete objects where isolation is natural.

**Recommended replacement: `chug`**

- Short-u, CVC, word-initial ch
- Picturable: a car engine, a train, a bottle of water being gulped — "chug" is onomatopoeia for a rhythmic, gulping/puffing sound
- Vocabulary familiarity: moderate — the sound is familiar even if the English label is less established; the picture + audio scaffold handles this

Alternative replacements if `chug` picture is unclear: `chum` (short-u, "friend" — picturable as two figures together; moderate familiarity) or `chuck` (short-u, to throw — action-based, moderate picturability).

**Recommended alternative if `rich` picture also fails:** `chick` (short-i, CVC, word-initial) — a baby chick/chicken is unambiguously picturable, vocabulary is high-familiarity (Tagalog "sisiw"), and it avoids the abstract-concept picturing problem. `chick` is a cleaner pick than `rich` and should be the backup.

### §4 — Words and patterns to avoid

#### §4a — Words with ch = /k/ (Greek/Latin loans)

Avoid any word where `ch` represents /k/, not /tʃ/. These words would teach the wrong phoneme for the ch grapheme or introduce a "ch can say /k/" complication that exceeds the tier's scope:

- Christmas, school, anchor, chorus, chronic, stomach, monarch — all ch=/k/
- Chef, champagne, charade — ch=/ʃ/ (French loans)

None of these are CVC words and none would appear in a CVC-level pool, but Kyle's distractor selection should also exclude c-initial distractors where the letter `c` says /k/ alongside ch-target words in the same session.

#### §4b — Words with silent letters or blends adjacent to ch

Avoid `scratch`, `screech`, `touch`, `couch`, `pouch`, `ouch` — these add blend complexity or vowel team complexity beyond the tier scope.

Avoid `catch`, `fetch`, `witch`, `watch`, `botch` — these use the `-tch` trigraph pattern (an alternative spelling of /tʃ/ after short vowels). The `-tch` spelling rule (use `-tch` after a short vowel at word end; use `-ch` elsewhere) is a separate phonics lesson and should not appear in the first ch pool, which teaches `ch` as the standard grapheme.

**Source 10 — Teach to Love Learning. "How I Teach TCH vs. CH (Without Confusing Students)." https://teachtolovelearning.com/how-i-teach-tch-vs-ch-without-confusing-students/**

Weak evidence (practitioner resource). Confirms that the `-tch` vs. `-ch` spelling rule is a distinct lesson that should follow, not accompany, the initial `ch` introduction. "Mixing the two in the same lesson creates confusion about which grapheme the student should use." The ch pool should be entirely free of `-tch` words.

#### §4c — Words with long vowel or r-controlled vowel in ch-onset

Unlike the sh tier, the ch word stock is rich enough in short-vowel options that no long-vowel or r-controlled words are needed to reach 7-word pool depth. Avoid: `cheese` (long-e), `chain` (long-a), `chair` (air-vowel), `church` (r-vowel), `child` (long-i), `cherry` (r-vowel + complex). These are common in oral vocabulary but outside the phonics scope.

`cheese` and `chain` are the most tempting inclusions because vocabulary familiarity is high (both common in Filipino-English daily speech). Resist: the vowel patterns are outside formal instruction, and hybridMode scaffolding should be reserved for exceptional cases (as it was with sh tier), not used as a workaround to include familiar words. A pool of 7 strong short-vowel ch words is sufficient.

#### §4d — Abstract/unpicturable words

Beyond `much` (already flagged), avoid: `each`, `such`, `which`, `reach`, `teach`, `choose` — all are either function words, abstract concepts, or contain out-of-scope vowel patterns.

### §5 — Sequencing rationale: why ch follows sh, and what sh mastery assumes going in

This section briefly confirms the parent research note's recommendation (digraph-acquisition-marian.md §Q2) as it applies to the specific ch content.

**The sh-first rationale, confirmed:**

The sh tier established three things that the ch tier now builds on:

1. **The two-letters-one-sound concept is no longer new.** Emma introduced it for sh ("These two letters work together — they make one sound"). When ch arrives, Emma can say "Remember how sh made one special sound? Ch is the same — two letters, one sound!" The concept is a review, not a new lesson.

2. **The digraph-word format is familiar.** The chip-tap interaction with a digraph-word target (hear the word, find the chip) is the same as for CVC words — Marian has practiced it for 7 sh-tier words. No format novelty.

3. **The sh/ch minimal pairs are now pedagogically available.** `chip`/`ship` and `chop`/`shop` are usable discrimination anchors only because Marian has already practiced `ship` and `shop`. This is the specific payoff of the sh → ch sequence.

**What sh mastery gate means for ch dispatch:**

Per the parent research note's §Q6 recommendation: "begin mixing sh and ch together once both are at ~70%+ accuracy, before introducing th." This means ch introduction should begin when sh is progressing but not necessarily at 90% — the cross-digraph interleaving phase comes after both are partially established, not after either is fully mastered. The planner's session-composition decision about when to begin introducing ch words alongside sh words is separate from when to open the ch-tier node. The node opens when sh has had its 3-5 introduction sessions and Marian's sh accuracy is trending upward (~50%+ is the opening threshold; no need to wait for 90%).

**The question of what sh "mastery" means before ch opens:**

Strict OG sequencing would say: wait for 90% on sh before introducing ch. This is appropriate for classroom instruction with multiple children. For a one-child adaptive app, the more productive posture is: begin ch introduction in isolation at the same time the planner begins mixing sh into review sessions. The two phases — ch introduction (blocked) and sh review (consolidating) — can run in parallel within a session (no more than 2-3 ch introduction items; remaining items from mastered CVC and reviewing sh). This is consistent with the `phonics-sequence-marian.md` §Q5 session-structure recommendation.

## Risks / counter-evidence

### 1. Ch-pool is fully short-vowel — is the pool deep enough at 7 words?

The sh tier's depth came partly from including 3 long-vowel/r-controlled hybrids (`shoe`, `sheep`, `shark`). The ch tier has no hybrids, so the pool of 7 is entirely short-vowel. Is 7 enough variety for 3-5 introduction sessions?

Yes, with a narrower repetition range. Per `phonics-sequence-marian.md` §Q5, 2-3 new items per session over 3-5 sessions means approximately 7-10 unique words provide variety without forcing excessive repetition. A pool of 7 provides adequate variety: the planner can draw 2-3 from the 7 per session, rotating through the pool twice across 3-4 introduction sessions. This is the same depth calculus that validated the sh tier at 7 (after dropping `shore`). The ch tier does not need more words; it needs well-chosen ones.

If Kyle's spec review finds that 7 is too thin for some reason (e.g., if `chat` and `chest` fail picture-grounding review), the backup additions are `chap` (short-a), `rich` (short-i, word-final, if picture works), or `chum` (short-u). Any of these extend the pool to 8-9 without leaving the short-vowel domain.

### 2. The /tʃ/ → /ts/ substitution is production-only — does it affect recognition?

In the chip-tap format, the risk is not production (Marian saying "tsip" instead of "chip") but perception: does Emma's audio /tʃ/ reliably reach Marian as /tʃ/, or might she hear it as /ts/ and therefore not map it to the `ch` grapheme?

The answer is: perception is not the problem for /tʃ/. The IPA journals cited above confirm that Filipino English speakers at mesolect level perceive and produce /tʃ/ correctly in familiar loanwords. The perceptual mapping from Emma's Azure TTS `/tʃ/` to Marian's existing `/tʃ/` representations should be robust. This contrasts with /ʃ/, where the perception gap (no Tagalog phoneme in that spectral range) was more acute.

### 3. Word-final ch words are CVCC — does this add complexity?

For the chip-tap recognition format, CVCC vs. CVC is less consequential than it would be for decoding or spelling. The child sees the text chip `rich`, hears Emma say "rich," and taps the chip. The CVCC pattern's complexity (two consonants at word end) is encoded in the visual form but the child does not need to segment it for the recognition task. It is safe to include word-final ch words in the pool.

However: if the ch tier eventually supports a "read the word" decode task (not just chip-tap recognition), `rich` and `much` would add a new element (CVCC decoding). The recommended 7 (all word-initial ch) avoids this complexity entirely. If Kevin or Devon's implementation of the ch content tier adds a decode task type, the word-final words should be annotated accordingly or excluded from decode-type sessions.

### 4. Sh/ch discrimination in the app — can the distractor matrix handle it?

The recommended pool includes `chop` and `chip` as minimal-pair targets alongside the sh-tier's `shop` and `ship`. This is pedagogically valuable, but it requires the distractor system to handle cross-tier minimal pairs. Specifically: if a ch-tier session selects `chip` as the target, should `ship` (from the sh tier) appear as a distractor?

For early ch introduction sessions: no. `ship` as a distractor for `chip` tests sh/ch discrimination, which should come in the later interleaving phase (when both tiers are at ~70%+), not in the initial ch introduction (where the task is "recognize ch words," not "discriminate sh from ch"). The distractor pool for initial ch introduction should be from the ch pool itself plus CVC words — not from the sh pool. When the planner begins interleaving sh and ch, cross-pool distractors become appropriate.

This is a planner-side annotation concern; Kyle's word-list spec should note the timing of cross-pool distractor availability.

### 5. Limited Tagalog-specific data on ch acquisition at age 8

The sociolectal research cited (Sources 2, 3, 4) documents adult and educated-speaker patterns, not specifically 8-year-olds in home-instruction contexts. The prediction that Marian has "better /tʃ/ access than /ʃ/ access" is structurally grounded (loanword phonology argument) but not empirically verified for this age group and context. Thomas's observation of Marian's sh responses should calibrate expectations for ch: if she found sh harder than predicted (more /s/ substitution than expected), ch will likely be easier. If she found sh easier than expected (already had some /ʃ/ access), ch difficulty is harder to predict.

## Non-obvious findings

1. **The ch tier has zero hybridMode words — this is structurally unlike the sh tier and should be stated explicitly in Kyle's spec.** The sh tier's 3 hybrids were a necessary exception driven by vocabulary familiarity (shoe, sheep, shark) outweighing pool-restriction concerns. The ch tier has no such trade-off: the short-vowel ch word stock is rich enough (chin, chip, chop, chat, chest, chug, chick, chap, chum, chuck, rich...) that long-vowel inclusions are never necessary. Stating this explicitly prevents future misapplication of the hybridMode pattern to ch.

2. **The c-says-k orthographic interference is ch's unique pedagogical burden.** Every other digraph introduced so far (sh) had no prior grapheme conflict: Marian had not learned that `s` and `h` mean anything separately before sh was introduced. But she has learned that `c` says /k/ from her CVC instruction (cat, cup, cap, cop). When `ch` arrives, the c-letter's prior mapping creates a hypothesis she must explicitly override. This is a one-time metalinguistic move that Emma's introduction script must make clearly. It does not require additional screen design — just the right script line. Kyle's spec should include it as a mandatory intro-script element.

3. **The sh/ch cross-pool minimal pairs (chip/ship, chop/shop) are a planned discrimination asset, not a collision.** The fact that two ch-pool targets have sh-pool counterparts should be flagged as a feature of the sh-first sequencing, not a problem to avoid. When the interleaving phase begins, these pairs are the highest-value discrimination targets. The word-list spec should annotate `chip` and `chop` with a note like "minimal pair: ship/shop (sh-tier)" so future planner and canon work can leverage these pairs intentionally.

4. **Ch-final words (rich) introduce CVCC pattern for the first time in the digraph tiers.** The sh tier's final words (shell — CCVC, shed — CVCC-ish) used sh-initial position. `rich` is the first recommended word where the digraph appears at word-end in CVCC position. If Kevin's planner impl ever adds a decode task type for the ch tier, `rich` would need special handling (the digraph is the coda, not the onset — a different visual pattern). For the current chip-tap format this is invisible, but it should be annotated in Kyle's spec as a future-extensibility note.

5. **Implication for the planner's 3-place sync contract:** `digraphs-ch` needs the same three-place update that `digraphs-sh` required when first-class content shipped: (1) `WORD_SONG_FIRST_CLASS_FOCUS_NODES` in `api/_planner.ts`, (2) `WORD_SONG_FOCUS_NODES` in `scripts/generateSessionCanon.ts`, and (3) the combo-count assertion in `scripts/generateSessionCanon.test.ts`. This is no different from any other new first-class tier, but it is worth flagging explicitly since the 14-place widening contract checklist (`sibling-tier-checklist.md`) covers the SkillNode infrastructure — the 3-place content-sync contract for first-class planner support is documented separately in `planner-and-canon.md` and should be part of Kevin's impl ticket AC.
